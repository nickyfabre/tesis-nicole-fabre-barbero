# 5. Discussion and Conclusions

The results show that shared peptides should not be discarded, since on the tree they give correct clade totals and, split among strains, they correct the strains that depend on them. Iterating the split improved the estimates only when many peptides were shared, and weighting each identification by its probability of being correct was able to replace the confidence threshold, especially for low-abundance organisms.

## 5.1 Shared peptides (H1)

The first hypothesis proposed that assigning shared evidence to the lowest common ancestor of the species that contain it gives more complete estimates than excluding it. At the clade level, the results support it: discarding shared peptides removed almost all of the signal of the clades formed by related strains, whereas keeping them at the LCA placed those clades close to their true value. At the species level, however, the hypothesis needs to be refined. Leaving the signal at the ancestor does not change any leaf, so strains whose signal is almost entirely shared remain as underestimated as if that signal had been discarded. Only splitting it among the candidate strains corrects them. Keeping the signal on the tree makes it possible to estimate how much belongs to each clade, and the split makes it possible to distribute that amount among the strains that form it. Because a shared peptide is only split among strains below its LCA, the split never changes the clade total.

This distinction is important because the LCA is the usual way of handling shared evidence in metaproteomics. MEGAN [@huson2007] and Unipept [@mesuere2016webservices] assign each ambiguous sequence to the LCA of its matches, and most pipelines adopt this criterion [@alves2025; @holstein2026]. For identification the LCA is a sound rule, because it never claims more resolution than the evidence allows. For species-level quantification, however, it is incomplete, since an explicit decision on how to divide the ancestor's signal is needed.

The split separated strains that identification-based methods cannot tell apart. The two *Rhizobium* strains of the mock community share almost all their peptides. @kleiner2017 could not resolve them with protein inference, and Peptonizer2000 reported two *Rhizobium* species as present in the same community because the detected peptides do not distinguish them [@holstein2026]. In the community analysed, the few unique peptides of each strain were enough to anchor the split and correct the estimate of both in all three designs.

The idea of using shared peptides has precedents. @pible2020 showed that the pattern of peptides shared between organisms carries quantitative information, and in metagenomics Bracken redistributes to species the reads that Kraken could only assign to a higher rank [@lu2017; @wood2014], analogous to the Proportional arm. What this thesis adds is an evaluation of each of these components separately against a known composition.

In the mock community the effect was concentrated in four strains, because shared peptides carry only 8 % of the intensity and almost all of them belong to three strain pairs. Most strains therefore changed little and the test over all strains was not significant, although the largest errors disappeared. The simulations show that the effect grows with the proportion of shared peptides, and real communities with many related strains, such as the human gut, can be expected to be closer to that scenario.

Iterating the split with EM changed nothing in the mock community, because with few shared peptides the Proportional starting point is already close to the solution. In the simulations, EM was better only with many shared peptides. Since it has no practical cost and does not require every strain to have unique peptides, it is a reasonable default. This estimator is the one used in RNA-seq for reads that map to several transcripts [@li2011] and the one used by MiCId in metaproteomics [@alves2025; @alves2026], which also lets internal nodes keep part of the signal. In the simulations, this all-rank model gave the same clade totals as the leaf-only model, but with many shared peptides it kept signal at internal nodes and lost resolution at the species level. This result corresponds to a complete database. When the sample contains organisms missing from the database, whose peptides can only be explained by a higher rank, signal at internal nodes may be useful (Section 5.3).

## 5.2 PEP weighting (H2)

Metaproteomic allocation methods, including those that split shared peptides, quantify only the identifications that pass a threshold and treat them as correct. Peptonizer2000 [@holstein2026] does use the confidence of each peptide, but to decide which taxa are present, not how much of each there is. No published method was found that uses the PEP of each identification to weight its contribution to the abundance of organisms. The idea follows the principle of Triqler [@the2019]: identification uncertainty is propagated into quantification instead of being removed with a filter. With weights, uncertain identifications contribute less signal instead of being removed, and a threshold is no longer needed. Because the weight acts on the evidence and not on the allocation model, it can be added to any allocation method; in the simulations it had the same effect on the leaf-only and the all-rank models.

The second hypothesis proposed that this weighting gives more accurate abundances than a threshold, in particular for low-abundance organisms. The results support it in two of the three designs, and show that it is the weights that make it safe to use every identification: without them, false signal on absent strains rose tenfold. The gain was concentrated in low-abundance strains. This can be explained because, in these data, the confidence of an identification depends on its intensity, so a strict threshold discards proportionally more evidence from rare organisms and underestimates them. @kleiner2017 observed the same bias, since all their approaches underestimated low-abundance species. The simulations reproduce the mechanism: when confidence did not depend on intensity the threshold was enough, and when it did, weighting clearly reduced the error of rare species. A benefit is therefore expected when, as in the real data, rare organisms are identified with lower confidence. Although the analysis by abundance was not planned and is reported as exploratory, the result held when the strains absent from other designs were excluded and agreed with the simulation.

The cost of using every identification is a small increase in signal on absent strains. Weighting reduces how much a false identification contributes, but not which strain it is assigned to, so on a truly absent strain any positive weight produces some signal. This effect is inherent to the estimator and does not depend on the calibration of the PEP. Moreover, the strain that receives the most false signal, Nm1, receives it from high-confidence identifications, with or without weighting. For estimating the abundance of organisms that are present but rare, the balance between cost and benefit is favourable. For deciding whether an organism is present, abundance should be combined with a presence criterion, such as the taxon-level probability computed by Peptonizer2000.

The method requires calibrated PEPs. In this dataset, Percolator's PEP, computed for individual spectra, underestimated false identifications at the peptide level, and had to be re-estimated from the decoys (Section 3.2). If the PEPs are not well calibrated, the weights act as a hidden threshold. Calibration should therefore be checked before applying the method, which is simple if the output keeps the decoys. For the same reason, on a table already filtered at 1 % FDR weighting does not change the results, because after the filter almost no uncertain identifications remain.

## 5.3 Limitations

The mock community is the only real dataset analysed; it has few shared peptides and only four strains could benefit from the split, too few for a statistical test. The simulations extend these conditions, but they only reproduce what was built into the simulator. In addition, the unit of analysis is the strain, which limits the observations to 26–30 per design and makes the conclusions depend on the strains of this community.

All strains were in the database. In real samples the database is often incomplete or contains relatives instead of the organisms present, and this choice strongly affects identification and taxonomic assignment [@tanca2013]. In this same community, when one *Rhizobium* strain was removed from the database, part of its signal moved to its close relative [@kleiner2017]. A model that splits among database strains will assign the signal of a missing organism to its relatives, and which model is preferable in that case was not evaluated.

Finally, no allocation method can separate organisms that share all their peptides, such as the three *Salmonella* strains that @kleiner2017 treated as one; for such groups the meaningful unit is the tree node. Errors in the measured quantity are not corrected either: the Enterobacteriaceae clade was underestimated by every arm because LT2 already was, a quantification problem that @kleiner2017 also described for the Uneven design.

## 5.4 Future work

-   **Differential abundance at tree nodes.** The original plan included a differential test between conditions at the tree nodes, which was not carried out. Methods such as treeclimbR [@huang2021], which choose the resolution of hierarchical hypotheses from the data, are a starting point. The tree should be used to place evidence rather than to share information between taxa, since smoothing along the phylogeny did not improve detection in microbiome studies [@bichat2020].
-   **Integration into Triqler.** The original plan proposed extending Triqler [@the2019]; during the work, the allocation problem turned out to be the central unsolved part. Integrating the allocation into Triqler would give a posterior distribution for the abundance of every node and a probabilistic differential test between conditions.
-   **Better PEPs.** Since the method depends on calibrated PEPs, rescoring tools such as MS2Rescore [@buur2024] could directly improve the abundances.
-   **Real samples and incomplete databases.** The next step is to apply the method to real communities, where shared peptides are much more frequent, and to evaluate what happens when the true organism is missing from the database, for example by removing proteomes from a mock community. That is also the setting in which to compare the leaf-only and all-rank models again.
-   **Software.** The allocation is implemented in the `taxoshare` library, which takes the quantMS output [@dai2024]. Building the tree from the taxonomy of the search database would make the workflow fully automatic for any dataset described with SDRF [@dai2021sdrf].

## 5.5 Conclusions

1.  Shared peptides should be kept: placed at the lowest common ancestor, they give correct clade totals, whereas discarding them underestimates clades of related strains.
2.  For species abundances, the shared signal must also be split among the candidate strains; this corrects the strains that depend on shared peptides without changing the others.
3.  Iterating the split with EM gives the same result as a single step when few peptides are shared and improves it when many are.
4.  Weighting each identification by 1 − PEP can replace the confidence threshold: it was more accurate in two of the three designs, especially for low-abundance strains, at the cost of a small increase in signal on absent strains. This use of identification confidence for estimating abundance is the main contribution of this thesis.
5.  Weighting requires calibrated PEPs and an unfiltered search, and its benefit is largest when confidence depends on intensity, as in the real data.
