# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Microbiomes and metaproteomics

The microbiome is defined as a characteristic microbial community occupying a reasonably well-defined habitat with distinct physico-chemical properties. The term refers not only to the microorganisms involved but also to their theatre of activity, which results in the formation of specific ecological niches. The term "microbiota" refers to the living organisms in a given microbiome [@berg2020]. Microbiomes are made up of bacteria, archaea, bacteriophages and other viruses, fungi and protozoa, and their taxonomic composition is highly diverse. In most cases they are highly structured in both membership and function, and there is growing evidence of their importance in virtually all processes of the biosphere [@vandenbossche2025]. Therefore, to understand a microbiome and its interaction with its environment or host, it is necessary to know which organisms make it up and in what proportions, as well as what functions they perform.

The term "metaproteomics" was first used for the large-scale characterization of the entire protein complement of environmental microbiota at a given point in time [@wilmes2004]. Mass spectrometry-based metaproteomics determines the taxonomic composition of microbiomes while offering insights into the relative biomass of each taxon, because it reflects the protein content of each taxon within a sample. It is a powerful, high-throughput method for studying microbiomes that enables the direct identification and quantification of biological functions, whereas metagenomics and metatranscriptomics provide insight only into potential functions and gene expression [@alves2025]. Metaproteomics comprehensively analyzes the proteins expressed within a microbiome, quantifying their abundance and characterizing their modifications, interactions and localizations. As the primary catalytic and structural elements of the cell, proteins directly reflect the microbiome's phenotype [@vandenbossche2025].

## 1.2 Proteins as a measure of biomass

Metaproteomics enables the study of the functions of environmental microbiomes, and also of their composition. Since proteins make up a large part of the cellular dry mass [about 55 % in *Escherichia coli*, @neidhardt1996], they are excellent estimators of microbial biomass [@kleiner2017]. Using mock communities of known composition, @kleiner2017 showed that metaproteomic estimates of the proteinaceous biomass of each species agree better with the true community composition than estimates from 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing or from metagenomics. Consequently, protein abundance provides a more direct proxy for biomass than gene-centric approaches. However, their study also identified the main difficulties of the approach: abundances of species present in low amounts are underestimated, quantification depends on how proteins are inferred from peptides, and closely related strains are hard to distinguish [@kleiner2017].

## 1.3 From spectra to organisms: identification and its uncertainty

In shotgun proteomics, the most common proteomic method, proteins are digested into peptides, which are separated by liquid chromatography and analysed by tandem mass spectrometry. In each measurement cycle the instrument first records the intact peptide ions (the MS1 spectrum); the intensity of a peptide in this spectrum is the signal used to quantify it. It then isolates and fragments selected ions and records their fragment spectra (MS2 spectra), which are used to identify them [@vandenbossche2025]. The peptides are identified by comparing the measured fragment spectra with the theoretical spectra of peptides from a protein sequence database, and proteins are then inferred from the identified peptides [@nesvizhskii2005; @kleiner2017]. In metaproteomics the database contains the proteomes of many organisms, so the same steps also determine which organisms each peptide can come from.

Every peptide-spectrum match (PSM) is uncertain. Its reliability is usually controlled with a target--decoy search, in which the spectra are also searched against reversed or shuffled sequences that cannot be present, so that the number of decoy matches estimates the number of false target matches. From this comparison two quantities are derived: the false discovery rate (FDR) of a list of identifications above a score threshold, and the posterior error probability (PEP) of each individual PSM, that is, the probability that this particular match is wrong [@the2019].

Accurate protein quantification is difficult in label-free shotgun proteomics because of the multiple sources of error in the method. Traditional pipelines control these errors through a series of filtering steps, typically keeping only identifications below 1 % FDR. However, the filtered results are then often treated as completely correct, ignoring the remaining uncertainty and discarding the evidence below the threshold. Such simplifications can compromise FDR control and lead to misleading interpretations. To address this problem, the Bayesian model Triqler quantifies and propagates multiple sources of error explicitly, from identification to differential abundance, and postpones filtering until the final step. Compared with traditional workflows, which make overconfident point estimates after heuristic filtering, Triqler achieves greater sensitivity and more accurate FDR control by relying on probability distributions [@the2019]. Triqler has also been shown to integrate with and outperform conventional methods for data-independent acquisition [@truong2023]. This principle, that identification uncertainty should be propagated to the final result rather than filtered away, has not yet been applied to the estimation of organism abundances in metaproteomics.

## 1.4 The shared-peptide problem

Metaproteomics enables the characterization and quantification of proteins from microbial communities and can therefore be used to estimate the relative proteinaceous biomass contributed by different microorganisms [@hettich2012; @vandenbossche2025]. However, accurate biomass estimation remains challenging because quantitative information is initially obtained at the peptide level and must subsequently be assigned to proteins and taxa. Even in the proteomics of a single organism, the same peptide sequence can occur in several proteins. Deciding which proteins are present from this kind of evidence is known as the protein inference problem [@nesvizhskii2005]. In metaproteomics the problem extends across organisms, because the same peptide can also occur in proteins of different species. Bacterial genomic homology generates considerable peptide redundancy through shared sequences among orthologous and paralogous proteins, limiting protein inference and taxonomic assignment, particularly among closely related species [@levimortera2019]. Consequently, metaproteomic workflows commonly use protein grouping or taxonomic assignment strategies such as the lowest common ancestor (LCA) approach to handle shared peptide evidence. Although these methods help resolve ambiguous identifications, they can result in assignments at higher taxonomic levels and therefore limit the recovery of species-specific information [@alves2025].

In the LCA approach, introduced for metagenomic reads in MEGAN [@huson2007] and used for peptides by Unipept [@mesuere2016webservices], a sequence that matches several taxa is assigned to their lowest common ancestor in the taxonomy, that is, to the most specific taxon that contains all of them. The rule is conservative, because a peptide is never assigned to a more specific taxon than its evidence supports. For quantification, however, it leaves a question open: when the signal of a shared peptide stays at the ancestor, how much of it belongs to each of the organisms below? @kleiner2017 took a different route and quantified species from proteins inferred with strict criteria based on protein-unique peptides, which provides high specificity but uses only part of the evidence.

These limitations are particularly relevant when metaproteomic measurements are used to estimate microbial biomass, because the way shared peptide signals are assigned directly affects the inferred abundance of individual taxa. For closely related strains that share most of their detectable peptides, a strategy based on unique peptides alone uses only a small fraction of the available signal, and their abundance can then be strongly underestimated.

## 1.5 Approaches that use shared peptides

Several methods already use shared peptides instead of discarding them, or take into account the confidence of the identifications, that is, how likely each identification is to be correct (Section 1.3), although each of them solves only part of the problem.

The LCA approach described in the previous section keeps the evidence of shared peptides, but leaves it at the common ancestor: it shows which taxa are present, but does not divide the signal among them. @pible2020 went a step further and showed that shared peptides carry quantitative information. For each organism they described how many of its spectra also match other taxa as a function of phylogenetic distance, a kind of "signature" of the organism, and they explained the spectral counts observed in a mixture as a combination of these signatures. With this approach, which they called phylopeptidomics, they estimated the proportions of two closely related pathogens in artificial mixtures.

The MiCId workflow [@alves2025; @alves2026] divides the signal of shared peptides directly. It first decides which taxa are present and then splits the MS1 intensity of each shared peptide among the identified taxa that contain it, using an expectation--maximization (EM) algorithm: the signal of each peptide is divided in proportion to the current abundance estimates of its taxa, the abundances are recomputed, and the two steps are repeated until they no longer change. MiCId estimates abundances at every taxonomic rank, so that an internal node of the tree can keep part of the signal. On synthetic communities, this redistribution estimated biomass more accurately than Unipept and MetaGOmics [@alves2025]. The same principle is used in other fields: in RNA-seq, to divide reads that map to several transcripts [@li2011], and in metagenomics, where Bracken redistributes to the species the reads that Kraken could only classify at a higher rank [@lu2017; @wood2014].

The Peptonizer2000 [@holstein2026] is the method that comes closest to using identification confidence. It combines the score of every peptide, as reported by the search engine, with the peptide-to-taxon links of Unipept in a graphical model, and reports for each taxon the probability that it is present in the sample. Because it takes the uncertainty of each peptide into account, it can use less confident identifications: in a search against a very large reference database, changing the FDR threshold from 1 % to 5 % improved its results slightly, whereas those of Unipept became worse [@holstein2026]. However, this method estimates which taxa are present, not their abundance.

In all the methods that estimate abundance, identification confidence is handled with a threshold: only the identifications of a list whose FDR does not exceed 1 % are accepted (Section 1.3), and all accepted identifications are then treated as correct. The Peptonizer2000 is the exception, but it uses confidence only to decide which taxa are present. No method uses the probability that each identification is correct, its PEP, to weight its contribution to the abundance of organisms. Nor have the ingredients that these methods combine (keeping shared peptides on the tree, splitting them among organisms, iterating the split and accounting for identification error) been tested one at a time against a known composition, so it is not known how much each of them contributes. In this work these components are evaluated separately, and the PEP is proposed as a weight for abundance.

## 1.6 Benchmarking with communities of known composition

Although current quantification strategies can provide useful estimates of microbial abundance, their accuracy and reliability still require systematic benchmarking, that is, testing on data for which the correct answer is known, under controlled conditions [@vandenbossche2025]. In this context, mock microbial communities with defined compositions provide a controlled framework for evaluating whether metaproteomic measurements accurately recover known biomass contributions. Such benchmark datasets are therefore important for assessing the accuracy, reproducibility and reliability of quantitative approaches and for developing improved methods for metaproteomic biomass estimation [@vandenbossche2025].

The mock communities of @kleiner2017 are well suited for this purpose: about 30 strains of bacteria, archaea, a eukaryote and bacteriophages were combined in three designs with known protein amounts, including closely related strain pairs that share most of their peptides and strains deliberately left out of some designs.

Following this need for systematic benchmarking, the present study develops a method that places peptide abundances on a species tree and evaluates it on mock microbial communities of known composition, to assess the accuracy of species-level biomass estimation from metaproteomic data. By comparing estimated abundances with experimentally defined biomass contributions, this framework provides a controlled setting to evaluate how shared peptide information, and the uncertainty of each identification, can be used for quantitative taxonomic assignment.

# 2. Objectives and hypotheses

## General objective

To develop and evaluate a method that estimates the abundance of microbial species and strains from metaproteomic peptide data by allocating the signal of each peptide on a species tree, explicitly addressing peptides shared between organisms and the uncertainty of each peptide identification.

## Research question

How should peptides shared between organisms, and the confidence of each identification, be used to estimate the abundance of the species in a microbial community?

## Specific objectives

1.  To implement the allocation of quantMS peptide intensities onto a species tree, with five strategies of increasing complexity: Unique, Rigid LCA, Proportional, EM and EM + PEP.
2.  To evaluate these strategies on the mock communities of @kleiner2017 against their known composition, at the level of species and of internal tree nodes.
3.  To determine, with simulated communities, under which conditions of shared-peptide proportion and false-identification rate each component improves the estimates, and whether part of the signal should be assigned to internal nodes of the tree.

## Hypotheses

**H1.** When quantitative evidence maps to multiple closely related species, assigning this evidence to their most specific shared taxonomic level (their lowest common ancestor) yields more complete and reliable abundance estimates than excluding it, and should be preferred in species-resolved analyses.

**H2.** Weighting each identification by its probability of being correct (1 − PEP), instead of applying a fixed confidence threshold, gives more accurate abundance estimates, especially for organisms present at low abundance.
